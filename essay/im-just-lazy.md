# "I'm just lazy" is a verdict, not a cause

*Draft 8 — 10 August 2026*

A man sits on a bench looking at his phone. Fancy cars. A streamer making sound. A classmate who won the lottery — *lucky bastard*. Some children breaking pottery. The finger flicks.

The moon comes up. He goes home, crawls into bed, gets up to see another day. Artificial bread. Work.

A year goes by. He is not alone. He is with his phone.

That's a poem I wrote. I thought it was about a sad man in New York. It's a diagram.

Then I spent three days rebuilding it as an app, and killed the app, because by the end the app had become the thing the poem was about. That comes later.

You didn't waste four hours last night. You spent them. You just don't have anything to show for it.

Time in flow disappears. You look up, it's gone, you don't mind. Time spent scrolling drags, and then it leaves something behind. That's the feeling at midnight, when the evening went somewhere and you can't say where.

Then you say the thing. Usually not out loud.

*I'm just lazy.*

Four words, and they close the case.

## Nobody says they're afraid

You never hear *I'm afraid to make a website*. You hear *I couldn't be bothered*.

Those aren't the same claim. One is about a feeling in a moment. The other is about what kind of person you are. People say the second while the first is what's happening, because the second costs nothing to say.

And of everything here, it's the only part shaped like an identity.

A bad evening is an event. Events don't predict anything. *I'm someone who doesn't do things* is a general fact, and general facts apply in advance. That's what makes it stick. You're not explaining last night. You're explaining the next four hundred nights — and once you've done that, there's no reason left to test it.

This is the one part of the essay where I can point at something other than my own reasoning.

There's a line of experiments going back to the seventies where people get problems that can't be solved, then a solvable one afterwards. What predicts how badly they do on the second isn't the failure — it's the explanation attached to it. Mikulincer ran this several ways in the eighties: when failure got explained by something broad and lasting, the damage travelled to a task that had nothing to do with the first. Narrow or temporary explanations mostly stayed put. Diener and Dweck had found the same shape in children in 1978. The ones who fell apart were the ones citing lack of ability; the ones who didn't weren't more talented, they were busy on the problem instead of on themselves.

That isn't proof that saying *I'm lazy* costs you four hundred evenings — these are lab tasks with planted explanations. Take the narrower claim: whether you file a bad night as an event or as a fact about yourself isn't a neutral choice of words. It keeps turning up in what people do next.

So the verdict isn't the end of the story. It's what keeps the story running.

Walk back from it. You're bored. Consuming is easier than making, much easier, and it's built to be. So you consume. So you don't make anything. So you've got nothing to point at. So when you think about trying, there's no evidence you're any good, and now there's something real to be afraid of. So you don't. And you need to explain that, and *I'm just lazy* is sitting right there, free.

Then you're bored again.

That's the loop, and it's why *just try harder* is such a strange thing to say to someone standing inside it.

## An alarm with no address on it

Boredom feels like a hole, so we treat it like one — something to fill. I think it's closer to an alarm going off.

The best description I've found is Danckert and Elpidorou's: boredom as a signal that you've drifted out of a workable zone of engagement. What you're doing is asking too little of you, or too much, or it isn't landing as meaningful, and the feeling is that mismatch making itself heard.

Now notice what the alarm doesn't come with.

Instructions.

It tells you the current activity isn't working. It doesn't tell you what would. And a gap like that gets filled by whatever's nearest.

So *more* almost never helps. Tam and Inzlicht put numbers on one version of this in 2024: people who swiped and fast-forwarded between videos came out more bored than people who watched one thing the whole way through, having predicted the opposite about themselves. Their own reading is the part worth stealing — switching doesn't fail because the videos are bad, it fails because you never stay anywhere long enough for anything to mean anything.

So the alarm is real and it goes off on time. The apps just get there first — not because they're evil, but because they're the nearest thing to hand at the moment it rings, and it doesn't say where to go. You never find out what you'd have done if it had gone unanswered. The feed decides before you do.

Everyone who's tried to fix this goes after the same link. Block the app. Grey the screen. Lock yourself out for two hours. They pick that link because it's the cheapest to cut — you don't have to build anything to remove something.

But removing is subtraction, and subtraction doesn't travel. You get an empty fifteen minutes and the same boredom with nothing to put in it. Then you bypass it, not because you're weak, but because it's Thursday and the only thing between you and relief is a button.

There's a version of the same advice that doesn't even need an app.

> *"Get off your phone, you're wasting your life"*
> *"Go outside, it is so nice there."*
> No.

Everyone here is fighting for less. Less screen time, fewer hours, smaller numbers. Not one of them hands the time back as anything.

And all of them assume the phone is the thief. Keep that. It's the assumption that cost me three days.

## Discipline is bullshit

The version we get sold is, anyway. It has a shape, and the shape is the problem.

It says: pick a goal, then pay for it now. You choose the destination first and take the cost immediately — the grind, the forcing, the hours you don't want — on the promise that the destination shows up later.

The consequence is at the front. The reward is a rumour.

That's force aimed straight at what you actually want. Force needs a supply. Yours runs out, and it runs out on a Tuesday for no reason you can name, and then the whole thing collapses at once and you've got another entry for the pile.

Turn it around.

You don't get through a path in order to arrive at a success. You do something, and the success is what's left behind by having done it.

In the first, the goal causes everything and the present is the price. In the second, the present causes everything and the goal is the residue. And the second is far easier to run, for a reason that has nothing to do with willpower: a consequence doesn't need managing. It arrives on its own. It only needs you to keep doing the thing.

Now the objection, because it marks the edge of what I'm claiming.

Some things really do work the way discipline says. Exams. Scales. Revising electrolysis on a Wednesday night. The reward is a rumour and you have to do it anyway. Fine — that isn't what this is about.

And I should be exact, because there's a trapdoor here. I'm not talking about wanting a result. *I want to have written a story* is a want for a result, and results can genuinely need forcing; the process was never the appealing part. I'm talking about the other one: the urge to do the activity itself, already present, already yours, which gets diverted in the gap between arriving and acting.

*I want to write. It's nine o'clock. I don't write.*

Nobody needs an explanation for why they're avoiding electrolysis. That one's obvious. The strange case is the second, and discipline gets prescribed for both — which is why it does nothing there, and why failing at it feels like such damning evidence about you. You were force-feeding something that was never short of appetite.

So the question changes. It stops being *how do I force myself towards what I want* and becomes *what would I actually do on a Tuesday at nine*. You can answer that tonight, and then check.

I wrote this before I understood it:

> Now I have become the Will:
>
> I will remember what I wanted
> staring at the mirror in my bathroom —
> ticking off stories I had written
> on my forehead,
> when the clock strikes Have.

Will and Have. Two tenses, neither of them now. *I will* is the countdown, the deadline, the plan. *I have* is the badge, the certificate, the portfolio. Both pull you out of the only place anything gets made.

And what you have achieved keeps becoming ordinary. Having done it doesn't.

There's a hard edge to this and I don't want to soften it. You are free to do whatever you want. You are not free of the consequences. Nothing is owed and nothing is required, but the evenings still went somewhere, and something is still true at the end of the week.

## A verdict needs evidence

If *I'm just lazy* is a verdict, then the question isn't how to feel better about yourself. It's what overturns it.

Bandura's answer, steady since 1977: the belief that you can do something is assembled from four sources, and only one of them is made of your own experience — having done it before. That's the dependable one. Persuasion moves the needle and doesn't hold it there. Finishing something holds.

Except there's a catch, and it's the best objection to everything I've just said.

Diener and Dweck went back in 1980 to see what happens to these same people when they *succeed*. The helpless ones undercounted their successes. They didn't read them as evidence of ability. They didn't expect them to continue.

So the verdict doesn't merely survive counter-evidence. It processes it. You make the thing and then you hear yourself explaining it away — it was small, it was easy, anyone could have, it doesn't count.

That's why one good night overturns nothing, and why the only advice I trust here is so unglamorous. Not *make something impressive*. Make something small, and then do it again, because a single evening is easy to dismiss and a count is not. You can argue with a good Tuesday. Seven of them, written down, is more work to explain away than to accept.

And it isn't usually the work that's hard to start. It's the deciding — the blank first minute, file open, no idea what the first move is. So make that move mechanical. Open the file, paste the three quotes in, draw the four boxes. Something you execute instead of invent.

## I built an app for this. Then I killed it.

Three days of work. Sixty-two logged decisions, eighty-seven numbered requirements, thresholds, predictions written down in advance so I couldn't lie to myself about them afterwards. It was probably the most careful thing I've made.

Two things stopped it, and both are worth more than the app.

The first. The whole product turned on one claim: you make something, you show a stranger, they respond, and that response changes how you see yourself. I built a lot of machinery to guarantee the response, because silence is the internet's default.

Then someone asked me to describe times something had actually changed for me. I gave three. A teacher who was good at English, which led to me writing. Going outside and stopping completely. My stepfather showing me VS Code, and the two of us making something together.

None of them is *I made a thing, a stranger reacted, and it changed me.* Two of them are someone who could already do the thing sitting down next to me at the start. That's company, not feedback. The third had no other person in it at all.

I built a machine to manufacture an experience I have never had, out of a theory I believed instead of the evidence I already owned. It's one person and it proves nothing about anyone else, and it should still have stopped me on day one.

The second is worse. I started this after watching a video essay and wanting to make something too. That part was real. Then the wanting got handed to my personal statement to carry.

A project that has to be impressive before it's allowed to be real doesn't get made. It gets planned, endlessly, because planning is the good half and it feels like progress the entire time. Sixty-two decisions. Nothing built. And four screens deep inside the product I had banned things you *have* — badges, trophies, portfolio-as-achievement — on the grounds that they devalue and the doing doesn't.

The project was one. I couldn't see it because I was standing in it.

So here's a chain that appears in none of my eighty-seven requirements, and it's the only one I've watched from the inside:

consumption of something good → a real urge to make → the urge handed to a credential → nothing made.

The consumption wasn't the fault. A good video essay is not a feed, and what it did to me was exactly right: it made me want to make something.

The capture was the fault.

## Capture

Once you have that word, the essay gets bigger than phones.

I drew the loop wrong the first time. I had:

boredom → scrolling → nothing made → no evidence → I'm just lazy

Change one box:

boredom → capture → nothing made → no evidence → I'm just lazy

Capture isn't one mechanism. It's my name for one outcome arrived at several different ways, and I'd rather say plainly that this is a category I've drawn than pretend it's a thing someone has measured. What they share is only this: each resolves the immediate tension without producing the thing.

The feed resolves boredom with stimulation. Fear resolves the uncertainty by retreat — not acting settles the risk, immediately and completely. The credential resolves the need for the thing to matter by promising future approval instead. And planning resolves the pressure to begin with the feeling of progress, the most flattering of the four, because from the inside it's indistinguishable from work.

Different mechanisms. Same result. The tension goes away. The thing doesn't get made.

Fear turns up twice there, and that isn't a slip: it sits in the chain, because no evidence you're any good is what makes trying frightening, and it's also one of the ways capture happens, because not acting settles the fear on the spot. That's what tightens the loop instead of merely repeating it.

The credential one isn't quite a substitution, though. It's a conversion. *I want to make this* becomes *I want to have made something impressive*, and the urge moves out of doing and into having.

Which is the poem, two sections up. Will and Have. I had written the diagnosis before I had the diagnosis.

So consumption isn't the problem by itself. Capture is. Sometimes consumption does the capturing, and the video-switching research is a good picture of exactly that — but it's one route out of four, which is why blocking an app was never going to be the whole answer.

Mine wasn't even a phone.

## Two more things I got wrong

The first I got wrong in writing, which makes it checkable. I recorded, in the project's own log, that nobody involved could use it, and that everything I'd written about how making something feels was reasoned rather than felt. That was false, and I had the disproof sitting in a folder. The man on the bench with his phone. A poem that ends *what is even the purpose of trying*. Another about going outside to stand in one place like a freak, breathing. I had the felt thing the whole time. I wrote a market thesis instead, because a market thesis looked serious and a poem didn't.

Which is the same capture again, one floor down.

The second. I thought I was doing science — thresholds, predictions locked before the test, a written statement of what result would kill it. That was sincere and none of it was wasted. But it isn't what I was doing. I was trying to reproduce a feeling I'd had, the one where somebody who could do the thing sat down next to me and it became possible. That's not a hypothesis.

Art and science aren't the same, but they rhyme. Science asks whether a thing is true and will tell you no. Art asks whether it lands, and gets to be true in a way you can't check. Confusing them is how you end up with a research plan bolted to a feeling, which is roughly what I had. But both are one person making something and handing it to a stranger to find out, and in both, the handing over is the part you don't get to skip.

I had three days of preparation and no handover. This is the first thing anyone has actually been given.

## Not this

Boredom doesn't go away when you stop scrolling. It comes back undiluted, and it's unpleasant, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Anyone promising you the removal of that feeling is selling something.

But it isn't a fault. It's an alarm that's been intercepted for years and has finally got through, and all it says is *not this*.

I wrote a line once that I only understood later. *Grief means it mattered.* The bad feeling at midnight is the same shape. It hurts because you wanted something. If you'd wanted nothing you'd have felt nothing, and that would be worse.

So the verdict is disputable. But only with evidence — and against a claim about what you do, the evidence eventually has to contain things you did.

You are reading this instead of making something. I know. I wrote it instead of building the thing.

Boredom doesn't tell you to go and make something. It only tells you *not this*. I'm the one telling you the next part.

Go and make something small and bad tonight. Then it isn't a verdict any more. It's just a Tuesday you did something on.

- Diener, C. I. & Dweck, C. And *II: The processing of success*, JPSP 39(5), 940–952 (1980) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7441483/ — helpless children "underestimated the number of success… did not view successes as indicative of ability, and did not expect the successes to continue."

- Danckert, J. & Elpidorou, A., *In search of boredom: beyond a functional account*, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 27(5), 494–507 (2023) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36922277/ — boredom as an affective signal of deviation from an optimal "Goldilocks" zone of cognitive engagement, which notably does not prescribe the next action.
- Tam, K. Y.
