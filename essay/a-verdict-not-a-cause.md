# "I'm just lazy" is a verdict, not a cause

*Draft 2 — 6 August 2026*

A man is sitting on a bench looking at his phone. Fancy cars. A streamer making sound. A classmate who won the lottery — *lucky bastard*. Some children breaking pottery. The finger flicks.

Then the moon comes up and there's only redness in his eyes, so he goes home and crawls to bed and gets up to see another day. Artificial bread. Work.

A year goes by. He is not alone. He is with his phone.

I wrote that and thought it was a poem about a sad man in New York. It isn't. It's a diagram. I spent the next three days rebuilding it as a product without noticing I already had it.

Here's what the diagram is about.

You didn't waste four hours last night. You spent them. You just don't have anything to show for it.

Time in flow disappears. You look up, it's gone, you don't mind. Time spent scrolling drags, and then it leaves something behind. That's the feeling at midnight, when the evening went somewhere and you can't say where.

Then you say the thing. Usually not out loud.

*I'm just lazy.*

That sentence is the most interesting part of all of this, and almost nobody treats it properly.

## Nobody says they're afraid

You never hear *I'm afraid to make a website*. You hear **I couldn't be bothered**.

Those aren't the same claim. One is about a feeling in a moment. The other is about what kind of person you are. People say the second while the first is what's happening, because the second costs nothing to say.

And of everything here, it's the only part shaped like an identity.

A bad evening is an event. Events don't predict anything. *I'm someone who doesn't do things* is a general fact, and general facts apply in advance. That's what makes it stick. You're not explaining last night. You're explaining the next four hundred nights, and once you've done that there's no reason left to test it.

So the verdict isn't the end of the story. It's what keeps the story running.

Walk back from it. You're bored. Consuming is easier than making, much easier, and it's built to be. So you consume. So you don't make anything. So you've got nothing to point at. So when you think about trying, there's no evidence you're any good, and now there's something real to be afraid of. So you don't. And you need to explain that, and *I'm just lazy* is sitting right there, free, and it closes the question.

Then you're bored again.

The loop closes. That's why it doesn't resolve on its own, and why *just try harder* is such a strange thing to say to someone standing inside it.

Now, boredom. Boredom feels like a hole, so everyone treats it like one. It isn't. It's a signal, and what it means is that whatever you're doing right now isn't working and you should go do something different. Not better content. Different activity.

Which is why filling it doesn't help. The APA published work on this in 2024: people who swipe fast between videos, trying to get away from boredom, end up more bored than people who watch one thing all the way through. The escape makes the thing. More stimulation can't fix disengagement, because disengagement was never a shortage of stimulation.

So the signal is real and it's arriving. The apps just get to it first. You never reach the part where boredom does its job, which is to make sitting there unbearable enough that you get up.

Everyone who's tried to fix this goes after the same link. Block the app. Grey the screen. Lock yourself out for two hours.

They pick that link because it's the cheapest to cut. Removing is easy. You don't have to build anything to remove something.

But removing is subtraction, and subtraction doesn't travel. Cut consumption and the rest of the chain doesn't run backwards. You get an empty fifteen minutes and the same boredom with nothing to put in it. Then you bypass it, and not because you're weak. Because it's Thursday, you're bored, and the only thing between you and relief is a button.

There's a version of the same advice that doesn't even need an app.

> *"Get off your phone, you're wasting your life"*
> *"Go outside, it is so nice there."*
> No.

Everyone in this category is fighting for less. Less screen time, fewer hours, smaller numbers. Not one of them hands the time back as anything.

## Discipline is bullshit

The standard prescription for all of this is discipline, and discipline has a specific shape that's worth saying out loud, because the shape is the problem.

Discipline says: pick a goal, then pay for it now. You choose the destination first and take the cost immediately — the grind, the forcing, the hours you don't want — on the promise that the destination shows up later. The consequence is at the front. The reward is a rumour.

That's force aimed straight at what you actually want. Force needs a supply. Yours runs out, and it runs out on a Tuesday for no reason you can name, and then the whole thing collapses at once and you've got another entry for the pile.

Turn it around.

You don't get through a path in order to arrive at a success. You do something, and the success is the consequence of having done it.

That isn't motivational phrasing, it's a different plan. In the first one the goal causes everything and the present is the price. In the second the present causes everything and the goal is what's left behind. And the second is much easier to manage, for a reason that has nothing to do with willpower: a consequence doesn't need managing. It shows up on its own. It only needs you to keep doing the thing.

So the question changes. It stops being *how do I force myself towards what I want* and becomes *what would I actually do on a Tuesday at nine*. You can answer that honestly tonight and check.

I wrote this before I understood it:

> Now I have become the Will:
> I will remember what I wanted
> staring at the mirror in my bathroom —
> ticking off stories I had written
> on my forehead,
> when the clock strikes Have.

Will and Have. Two tenses, neither of them now. *I will* is the countdown, the deadline, the plan. *I have* is the badge, the certificate, the portfolio. Both of them pull you out of the only place where anything gets made. And the thing about *have* is that it devalues — a page of achievements is worth almost nothing in five years, and the hours you actually spent making things are worth the same as they ever were.

There's a hard edge to this and I don't want to soften it. You are free to do whatever you want. You are not free of the consequences. Nothing is owed and nothing is required, but the evenings still went somewhere, and something is still true at the end of the week.

If *I'm just lazy* is a verdict, then the question isn't how to feel better about yourself. It's what evidence overturns it.

Bandura's answer, and the research has been steady on this for decades: you believe you can do something mainly because you have done it. Not because you were told. Persuasion moves a little and doesn't hold; finishing something moves it properly. So every poster and product and well-meaning friend telling you you're capable is working the weakest channel available, and the thing that works is boring and unglamorous and made entirely of small finished things.

Make something and the confidence follows. Not because it feels nice. Because a verdict needs evidence, and *I'm someone who doesn't do things* runs out of it the moment there are things.

One more thing on this. If you're ever going to show someone what their time did, only show them the half they can't already see. Nobody needs telling that last night went nowhere. They were there. Boredom is time you feel passing, so that consequence was delivered in full, on the night, by the night. Saying it again isn't information. It's telling someone off with a progress bar attached.

What you genuinely don't know is that you came back to this on seven different days. Nobody counted. That's the only new fact available.

And the reason it's hard to start isn't the work. It's the deciding. The blank first minute, file open, no idea what the first move is — that's where things die. Not at minute forty when it gets hard. At minute one, when it's vague. So make the first move mechanical. Open the file, paste the three quotes in, draw the four boxes. Something you execute instead of invent. Invention can come at minute five when the thing is already moving.

## I built an app for this. Then I killed it.

Three days of work. Sixty-two logged decisions, eighty-seven numbered requirements, thresholds, predictions written down in advance so I couldn't lie to myself about them afterwards. It was careful. It was probably the most careful thing I've made.

Two things stopped it and both are worth more than the app.

The first. The whole product turned on one thing: you make something, you show a stranger, they respond, and that response changes how you see yourself. I built a lot of machinery to guarantee the response, because silence is the internet's default.

Then someone asked me to describe times something had actually changed for me. I gave three. A teacher who was good at English, which led to me writing. Going outside and stopping completely. My dad showing me VS Code, and the two of us making something together.

None of them is *I made a thing, a stranger reacted, and it changed me.* Two of them are someone who could do the thing sitting down next to me at the start. That's company, not feedback. The third had no other person in it at all.

I built a machine to manufacture an experience I have never had, out of a theory I believed instead of the evidence I already owned. It's one person, it proves nothing about anyone else, and it should still have stopped me on day one.

The second is worse. I started this after watching a video essay and wanting to make something too. That part was real. Then the wanting got handed to my personal statement to carry.

A project that has to be impressive before it's allowed to be real doesn't get made. It gets planned, endlessly, because planning is the good half and it feels like progress the whole time. Sixty-two decisions. Nothing built. And four screens deep inside the product I had banned things you *have* — badges, trophies, portfolio-as-achievement — on the grounds that they devalue and the doing doesn't. The project was one. I couldn't see it because I was standing in it.

So here's a chain that appears in none of my eighty-seven requirements, and it's the only one I've watched from the inside:

consumption of something good → a real urge to make → the urge handed to a credential → nothing made.

The consumption wasn't the fault. A good video essay is not a feed, and what it did to me was exactly right. The capture was the fault.

I also want to say what I got wrong about myself, because I got it wrong in writing. I recorded, in the project's own log, that nobody involved could use it and that everything I'd written about how making something *feels* was reasoned rather than felt. That was false and I had the disproof in a folder. The man on the bench with his phone. A poem that ends *what is even the purpose of trying*. Another one about going outside to stand in one place like a freak, breathing. I had the felt thing the whole time. I wrote a market thesis instead, because a market thesis looked serious and a poem didn't.

Which is the same capture again, one floor down.

And the last piece. I thought I was doing science — thresholds, predictions locked before the test, a written statement of what result would kill it. That was sincere and none of it was wasted. But it isn't what I was doing. I was trying to reproduce a feeling I'd had, the one where somebody who could do the thing sat down next to me and it became possible. That's not a hypothesis.

Art and science are not the same, but they rhyme. Science asks whether a thing is true and will tell you no. Art asks whether it lands, and gets to be true in a way you can't check. Confusing them is how you end up with a research plan bolted to a feeling, which is roughly what I had. But both of them are one person making something and handing it to a stranger to find out. And in both, the handing over is the part you don't get to skip. I had three days of preparation and no handover. This is the first thing anyone has actually been given.

---

Boredom doesn't go away when you stop scrolling. It comes back undiluted, and it's unpleasant, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Anyone promising you the removal of that feeling is selling something.

But it isn't a fault. It's a signal that's been intercepted for years and has finally got through, and what it says is go and do something.

I wrote a line once that I only understood later. *Grief means it mattered.* The bad feeling at midnight is the same shape. It hurts because you wanted something. If you'd wanted nothing you'd have felt nothing, and that would be worse.

So the verdict is disputable. But only with evidence, and only evidence you made, because that's the only kind that counts against a claim about who you are.

You are reading this instead of making something. I know. I wrote it instead of building the thing.

Go and make something small and bad tonight. Then it isn't a verdict any more. It's just a Tuesday you did something on.
